# Meadow Pals animation production bible

## Authority and purpose

This is the current production standard for Meadow Pals cartoons and animated
story material. It governs the moving image, character consistency, timing,
audio, asset bank, compositing and review process. The finished film remains
the final authority: a technically valid render still fails if the acting,
weight, continuity or visual appeal is poor.

The goal is not to make every frame move. The goal is to make every movement
feel chosen, readable and alive. A high output frame rate cannot rescue weak
poses, linear sliding, disconnected props or inconsistent drawings.

## What the history teaches us

### 1920s and early 1930s: readable shapes and rhythmic motion

Early rubber-hose animation used simple, rounded character construction,
flexible arcs, graphic silhouettes and movement closely synchronised to sound.
The designs were economical enough to redraw repeatedly. `Steamboat Willie`
(1928) is an important reference for synchronised sound and action, not a
template for copying Mickey Mouse's design. Sources:

- [Smithsonian: Mickey Mouse turns 90](https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/mickey-mouse-turns-90)
- [ACMI: Animating Mickey Mouse](https://www.acmi.net.au/stories-and-ideas/disney-animating-mickey-mouse/)
- [Adobe: rubber-hose animation](https://www.adobe.com/uk/creativecloud/animation/discover/rubber-hose-animation.html)

**Meadow Pals lesson:** simplify forms enough that they can be animated, use
arcs rather than mechanical straight-line travel, and let sound support the
rhythm. Do not use jointless rubber limbs as a blanket style. Bouncy's springs
are a specific physical design and must still obey compression, release,
weight and contact.

### 1930s to 1950s: acting, weight and the classical principles

The mature hand-drawn pipeline developed strong poses, anticipation, squash
and stretch, timing, spacing, slow-in and slow-out, arcs, follow-through,
overlap, secondary action, staging, exaggeration, solid drawing and appeal.
Disney's current description of hand-drawn production still centres on rough
acting poses, weight and mass, overlapping action, clean-up, on-model drawing
and deliberate inbetweens. Source:

- [Walt Disney Animation Studios: hand-drawn animation](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/hand-drawn-animation/)
- [John Lasseter: Principles of Traditional Animation Applied to 3D Computer Animation](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/afs/cs/academic/class/15462-f12/www/lec_slides/Lesseter.pdf)
- [Glen Keane: A System for Planning Animation](http://www.animationmeat.com/pdf/featureanimation/Glen_Keane_Animation.pdf)

**Meadow Pals lesson:** acting is decided in thumbnails, keys and breakdowns
before smoothness is added. The breakdown is not a neutral halfway drawing; it
describes how the character leaves one thought and enters the next. Cleanup
must preserve the acting while restoring the exact model.

### Exposure, timing and spacing

Cinema delivery is 24 frames per second. Animation may be exposed on ones
(one new drawing each frame), twos (one drawing held for two frames) or threes
(one held for three). The spacing between drawings creates acceleration,
deceleration and weight. Equal spacing creates constant, usually mechanical
motion. Physics references such as the odd-number spacing rule are useful for
falls and bounces, but character performance may exaggerate physics for
clarity. Source:

- [Physics of Timing for Animators](http://www.floobynooby.com/ICAN3211/Timing_Tutorial.pdf)
- [Tom Bancroft: timing charts](http://tombancroft.blogspot.com/2012/03/timing-charts-for-traditional-animation.html)

**Meadow Pals lesson:** default to 24 fps delivery and animation on twos. Use
ones for impacts, fast spring releases, rapid turns, smear transitions and
precise object contact. Use threes or longer holds for a deliberately quiet
pose only when eyes, mouth, ears, tail, breathing, camera or another layer
continues the performance. Never create intermediate frames by simple linear
translation and call that character animation.

### 1960s to 2000s Japanese animation: selective movement and strong cinema

Japanese television animation transformed production constraints into a
visual language. `Astro Boy` cut the average number of new drawings, while
using fast cuts, held drawings, in-picture movement and zooms. Later anime
used precise layouts, expressive key drawings, effects animation, strong
backgrounds and brief bursts of fluid motion. Fewer drawings can look
excellent when the chosen drawings, cuts and timing are excellent. The modern
Toei pipeline still separates storyboard, key animation, inbetweens,
background, colouring, effects, compositing, editing and sound. Sources:

- [BFI: The seeds of anime](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/features/seeds-anime-how-japanese-animation-arose)
- [Toei Animation: production process](https://corp.toei-anim.co.jp/en/company/animation_production.html)
- [Oxford Academic: Limiting Movement, Inventing Anime](https://doi.org/10.5749/minnesota%2F9780816675494.003.0001)
- [TIME: Spirited Away and the digital transition](https://time.com/6081937/spirited-away-changed-animation-studio-ghibli/)

**Meadow Pals lesson:** spend drawings where a change of thought, contact,
weight shift or comic beat needs them. A held drawing must be a strong drawing.
Use cutaways, close-ups, reaction holds, effects and sound to shape attention,
but never use camera movement to disguise missing character animation.

## The Meadow Pals production system

### 1. Script and sound first

1. Lock the scene purpose, emotional change and comic beat.
2. Record or synthesise the final narration and dialogue before final timing.
3. Mark breath, emphasis, pauses, phonemes, effects and intentional silence.
4. Build an animatic using storyboard drawings and the final voice track.
5. Approve timing from the animatic before producing finished cels.

Narration must remain a warm, original British male voice. It may evoke the
clarity, warmth and dry wit of classic British storytelling, but must not
imitate or clone a named performer. Music is optional per film. When a film is
specified as voice-and-effects only, the audio mix must contain no background
music bed.

### 2. Shot planning

Each shot receives a shot card containing:

- shot ID, start time, end time and purpose;
- camera size, angle, lens feel and screen direction;
- background and multiplane layers;
- character model IDs and scale references;
- keys, extremes, breakdowns, contacts and holds;
- exposure choice for each body part or layer;
- mouth cues, blinks, eye direction and dialogue accents;
- prop ownership, contact points and z-order;
- sound cues and review notes.

The storyboard is a blueprint, not a sequence of final illustrations. The
animatic decides the edit. The X-sheet decides what appears on every frame.

### 3. X-sheet and drawing exposure

All films deliver at **24 fps**. Default exposure rules:

| Material | Normal exposure | When to change |
|---|---:|---|
| Dialogue body acting | twos | ones around a sharp accent; threes for a chosen hold |
| Mouth shapes | ones or twos | follow audible consonants and vowel shapes, not every letter |
| Bouncy spring release | ones | begin on twos during compression, then switch to ones |
| Walk, hop and bounce cycles | twos | ones at contact/impact if the silhouette stutters |
| Fast object, smear or debris | ones | may hold the strongest impact drawing for two frames |
| Quiet reaction | threes/hold | keep eyes, blink, breath or secondary part alive |
| Background | hold | move only for an intentional camera or environmental action |

The output therefore contains 24 frames each second, but it does not require
24 unrelated drawings each second. A normal dialogue scene may use 8 to 12
whole-character drawings per second, with separately exposed mouth, eye, ear,
tail or hand layers. A fast spring action may use 18 to 24 drawings per second.

### 4. Pose-to-pose drawing order

1. **Thumbnails:** tiny, fast drawings that solve silhouette, emotion and
   rhythm.
2. **Keys/extremes:** the indispensable storytelling poses.
3. **Breakdowns:** drawings that define arcs, leading body part, path, drag,
   overlap and the character's change of thought.
4. **Timing test:** keys and breakdowns only, played against final audio.
5. **Inbetweens:** added according to timing charts, never placed as automatic
   geometric averages.
6. **Cleanup:** consistent line, volume, proportions, markings and attachment.
7. **Colour:** locked palette with no frame-to-frame drift.
8. **Composite:** shadows, effects, depth, camera and grade.

Straight-ahead drawing is allowed for fire, dust, paint, water, loose wool,
spring vibration and other effects, but the effect must begin from and return
to the planned character contact.

### 5. Character construction and model locks

Every reusable character needs:

- front, three-quarter, side and back turnarounds;
- neutral height in pixels at the master 720p field;
- head-to-body proportions and eye-line;
- expression sheet;
- hand/hoof and mouth library;
- contact poses, walk/hop cycle, anticipation and recovery poses;
- colour swatches and outline rules;
- attachment anchors for props and costumes;
- forbidden mutations.

#### Bouncy

- Yellow wool silhouette.
- Exactly two coil-spring legs.
- Exactly two feet, visibly attached to the springs.
- No ordinary animal legs, hidden extra limbs or changing coil count.
- Springs compress before launch, extend through acceleration, trail through
  the apex and absorb landing.
- The body maintains visual volume through squash and stretch.

#### Woolly

- Cream/white wool, natural lamb legs and no metal springs.
- Calmer movement arcs and slower timing than Bouncy.
- The brush remains inside the hoof grip and follows the hoof's arc.
- Woolly reacts to Bouncy before the audience is asked to notice the result.

### 6. Contact, weight and world rules

- Feet may not slide while planted.
- A seated pose must visibly contact the seat; never place a standing drawing
  over a stool and describe it as sitting.
- Held props inherit the hand or hoof transform and must pass behind/in front
  of the correct body layers.
- Airborne objects follow readable arcs and visibly land.
- Shadows shrink and soften with height, widen at contact and remain aligned
  with the light direction.
- A jump contains anticipation, release, airborne arc, impact and recovery.
- Secondary motion overlaps the main action; ears, tail, wool, scarf and loose
  items do not start and stop on the same frame as the torso.

### 7. Camera and limited-animation rules

Use a locked camera unless a move contributes story information. Do not add
pan-and-scan to create the appearance of animation. Multiplane moves are
allowed when foreground, character plane and background have motivated,
different speeds. A held composition may remain alive through eye direction,
mouth shapes, a blink, environmental effects and sound.

Cut on a change of thought or action. Preserve screen direction, eye-line,
prop position and background geography across cuts. The barn-side set remains
consistent unless a new angle is established clearly.

### 8. AI usage boundary

AI may create model sheets, approved key-pose candidates, backgrounds,
textures and cleanup references. It must not independently redraw consecutive
final frames. That causes identity wobble, changing anatomy, sliding details
and morphing backgrounds.

Approved process:

1. Generate a controlled sheet, not a separate prompt for every frame.
2. Select and clean a canonical drawing once.
3. Assign a stable asset ID and model metadata.
4. Animate with deterministic drawings, layers and exposure.
5. If machine inbetweening is tested, compare it with artist-planned
   breakdowns and reject any topology or silhouette error.

RIFE or optical-flow interpolation may be used for smoke, light, water,
camera plates or very small corrective gaps after review. It is forbidden for
faces, hands, spring topology, costume details, prop contact or key acting.

### 9. Asset bank

Every approved image is stored once and tagged with:

- immutable asset ID;
- character, version and view;
- action, emotion and pose phase;
- source sheet and crop rectangle;
- transparent bounds, ground point and centre of mass;
- eye, mouth, hand/hoof, prop and shadow anchors;
- intended exposure and compatible adjacent poses;
- palette hash and model-check status;
- licence/provenance and approval status.

Filenames are descriptive but metadata is authoritative. Generated contact
sheets and temporary renders belong in a preview/artifacts folder, not in the
canonical bank.

### 10. Toolchain

#### Production tools

- **OpenToonz** (BSD-3-Clause): X-sheet, raster/vector drawing, cleanup,
  palette, multiplane compositing and effects.
- **Rhubarb Lip Sync**: frame-timed mouth-cue extraction from final dialogue.
- **OpenTimelineIO** (Apache-2.0): shot and editorial timeline interchange.
- **FFmpeg**: deterministic frame assembly, sound mix and delivery encoding.
- **Meadow compositor**: explicit shot JSON, asset IDs, anchors, z-order,
  exposure and 24 fps rendering.
- **cartoonimator reference** (MIT): useful deterministic compositing and
  viseme patterns, but not a complete character-animation solution.

Downloaded source lives under
`Meadow Pals Animation Trial/production-system/vendor/production`.

#### Research-only tools

- **AnimatedDrawings** (MIT): skeleton and motion-retargeting experiments.
- **ECCV2022-RIFE** (MIT): carefully restricted interpolation experiments.
- **Skeleton-Driven Inbetweening** (MIT): promising bitmap inbetween research,
  not a push-button production stage.
- **LayerInbetween** (GPL-3.0, SIGGRAPH 2026): promising occlusion-aware line
  inbetweening, too new to become production authority without evaluation.

Downloaded source lives under
`Meadow Pals Animation Trial/production-system/vendor/research`. Research
tools may not silently enter the production render path.

**Excluded:** AnimeInbet code/data is non-commercial-only and therefore is not
part of the commercial Literacy Guide production stack.

### 11. Quality gates

A shot fails if any answer is no:

1. Do the keys read clearly as still silhouettes?
2. Does every pose preserve the character model and exact limb count?
3. Do feet, seat, hands/hooves and props visibly contact?
4. Does motion follow an arc with intentional spacing?
5. Is there anticipation before, and recovery after, a major action?
6. Do overlap and secondary parts use offset timing?
7. Are scale, palette, line quality and light continuous?
8. Does the cut preserve geography, eye-lines and prop state?
9. Does dialogue have intelligible lip timing without mouth chatter?
10. Does the shot communicate its idea without narration?
11. Has the actual exported movie been watched at normal speed with sound?
12. Has it also been checked frame-by-frame at every contact and pose change?

Automated checks should flag sudden changes in silhouette area, character
height, ground point, palette, prop anchor, limb count and frame exposure.
Automation finds candidates; visual review decides whether the motion works.

### 12. Delivery standard

- 1280 x 720 for current trials.
- 24 fps constant frame rate.
- 48 kHz audio.
- H.264 review master plus lossless PNG frame sequence.
- Safe action and title margins.
- No background music when the film brief specifies narration and effects
  only.
- Archive the script, storyboard, animatic, X-sheet, shot JSON, asset manifest,
  frames, audio stems, final mix and review master.

## The central rule

Do not try to manufacture life by increasing a number. Life comes from the
relationship between poses: intention, anticipation, path, spacing, contact,
overlap, impact, reaction and recovery. Frame rate presents that work; it does
not replace it.
