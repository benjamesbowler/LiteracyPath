# Story Bible — Part I: How to Write an Excellent Children's Book

**Status:** Canonical writing and editorial standard
**Version:** 2.0
**Date:** 3 August 2026
**Applies to:** Guided-reading fiction and nonfiction, picture books, Story Quests and
story-led games

This is the research-led craft standard for writing, revising and approving children's
books. It governs narrative shape, informational shape, reading-band control, human
voice, image–text meaning and exact narration. A book that breaks a **MUST** rule does
not ship.

Part II contains the separate Literacy Guide world and character canon.

The [human-writing evidence database](../guided-reading/HUMAN_WRITING_EVIDENCE_DATABASE.json)
contains short, lawfully sourced examples used to identify transferable craft moves. It
is evidence for editorial principles, not a bank of lines to imitate or copy.

## 1. What every children's book must do

Every fiction book MUST:

1. Give a recognisable character a concrete want, problem, need or question.
2. Make the story's driving change clear near the beginning, even when the text is so
   short that the illustration carries part of the orientation.
3. Let the protagonist act. Help may come from others, but the resolution must not simply
   arrive around a passive protagonist.
4. Link action, reaction and consequence so a child can explain why the next event
   happened.
5. Include a meaningful obstacle, mistake, surprise, social effect or failed attempt.
6. Build toward a turn: the protagonist notices, chooses, stops, tries differently or
   responds to what their action caused.
7. Resolve the central goal or problem on the page.
8. End with a visible changed state, payoff or landing, not a departure, future promise,
   abstract summary or stated lesson.

Every nonfiction book MUST:

1. Give the reader a concrete topic-question, observation or concept to follow.
2. Organise facts through one visible progression rather than a list of unrelated
   statements.
3. Add meaning on every page through sequence, cause, comparison, classification,
   change, scale, location or close observation.
4. Use precise, supportable words and images without turning uncertainty into fact or
   animals and objects into accidental fictional characters.
5. Close the inquiry by answering, synthesising, comparing, returning to the opening
   with new meaning, or showing why the information matters.

Every book MUST:

1. Use words, sentence length, book length and inference demands appropriate to its
   declared reading band.
2. Make the illustration carry real information while still allowing the printed words
   to be decoded rather than guessed.
3. Preserve established character, world and visual canon wherever those apply.
4. Give every Story Quest choice a visible and logically different consequence.
5. Supply word-for-word matching narration for every page or scene.
6. Sound natural when spoken and be worth reading without teaching labels, rewards or
   progress counters.

The fiction test is: a child should be able to say **who wanted or needed what, what
changed, what they did, what happened because of it and how it ended**.

The nonfiction test is: a child should be able to say **what the book helped them find
out, how the ideas were connected and what the ending helped them understand**.

## 2. The evidence behind the standard

These rules combine children’s-literature craft with evidence about how young children
understand and learn from narrative.

### 2.1 Proven principles

- **Goal, attempt and outcome are the backbone of comprehension.** Research on narrative
  comprehension identifies a character’s goal, attempts and outcomes as fundamental.
  Logically matched goal structures make stories easier to understand. An initiating event
  should create the story problem and the main goal should drive what follows.
- **Keep the goal object present.** Repeated visual or verbal presence of the thing a
  character wants helps children connect the goal to later attempts and consequences.
- **Strong narrative and emotional engagement matter.** The Department for Education
  recommends stories that elicit curiosity, excitement, laughter or empathy; sustain repeat
  readings; extend vocabulary; and use engaging, inclusive illustrations.
- **Re-reading adds depth.** Familiarity increases emotional engagement, supports retelling
  and gives children another encounter with language and character.
- **Connected text matters.** The US What Works Clearinghouse recommends that children read
  connected text every day to develop accuracy, fluency and comprehension.
- **Decodable means decodable.** Text for a beginning reader must match the grapheme–phoneme
  correspondences they have learned. Pictures must enrich meaning, not make the child guess
  an undecodable word.
- **Oral language supports literacy.** The Education Endowment Foundation finds strong
  evidence for purposeful speaking and listening, especially book discussion, vocabulary
  work and structured questions integrated with story reading.
- **Choice and interest support engagement.** National Literacy Trust guidance reports that
  agency and choice can increase motivation, effort and persistence in reading.
- **Pictures are narrative, not decoration.** Research using wordless picture books shows
  that young children build and demonstrate narrative comprehension from a coherent visual
  sequence. Every image therefore has to preserve cause, state and continuity.
- **A problem makes a story; a list of events does not.** Children’s author Joyce Dunbar’s
  BookTrust guidance stresses a problem, an obstacle, a turning point and a resolution.
  Every word must earn its place.
- **Pattern supports fluency only when meaning moves.** Across the human-writing evidence
  database, strong early readers establish an audible frame, change a meaningful element
  on each return and break, reverse or reframe the pattern at the turn or close.
- **Nonfiction needs a concept spine.** Strong information books create a reason to turn
  the page through question, sequence, comparison, classification, cause or close
  observation, then close the inquiry rather than merely stop listing facts.

### 2.3 Evidence-to-practice matrix

| Evidence or expert principle | What it means for a writer | Mandatory editorial test |
|---|---|---|
| Children understand a narrative through linked goals, attempts and outcomes | Establish a concrete want, let the protagonist try, and show what each attempt causes | Can a child point to the goal, an attempt and its outcome? |
| A visible goal object supports comprehension | Keep the missing, wanted or changing thing present in words or pictures | Does the story’s spine disappear for more than one beat without a reason? |
| Strong narrative invites emotional engagement and rereading | Build curiosity, humour, tension, empathy or wonder into the action itself | Would the story still be enjoyable without rewards or teaching labels? |
| Repeated readings deepen familiarity, retelling and language learning | Plant details, refrains and visual jokes that reward a second reading | Is there anything satisfying to notice or anticipate on rereading? |
| Connected text develops accuracy, fluency and comprehension | Let the child read meaningful sentences and stories, not only isolated drills | Does the educational target live inside coherent meaning? |
| Decodable text must match taught sound–letter knowledge | Control the code before adding “interesting” but inaccessible vocabulary | Can the intended reader decode every required word using taught knowledge? |
| Oral-language work is strongest when purposeful and integrated | Ask questions that help children predict, explain, compare or retell | Does a prompt deepen the story rather than interrupt it? |
| Agency can support motivation and persistence | Offer choices that affect action and consequence | Can the child describe what changed because of their choice? |
| Illustrations carry narrative information | Use images to show state, space, emotion, consequence and secondary action | Could the images be placed in the right order without reading the captions? |
| Picture-book language must be economical | Remove explanation that the action or image already communicates | Does every line advance action, character, mood, rhythm or comprehension? |
| Early-emergent A–C levels are defined through interacting text characteristics, not a child's age | Control print, syntax, vocabulary, pattern, ideas, illustrations and book features while declaring interest age separately | Does the book fit its reading band without making the subject or emotional experience babyish? |
| Human-authored early readers use repetition as anticipation with meaningful change | Establish an audible pattern, let each return add action or knowledge, and break or reframe it at the turn or close | If repeated lines are removed, does the story or concept progression lose something? |

### 2.4 What the evidence does not justify

Research must never be used to excuse weak writing. It does **not** mean:

- every story needs a question after every page;
- every emotion should be named;
- repetition may replace plot progression;
- a moral should be stated at the end;
- an age range may substitute for reading-band analysis;
- a list of accurate facts automatically forms a nonfiction book;
- a final movement or cheerful image automatically forms an ending;
- “educational” vocabulary may exceed the declared decoding level;
- a colourful picture can rescue incoherent causality;
- two buttons constitute meaningful agency;
- adult approval is a substitute for a child understanding or enjoying the story.

The evidence determines the learning conditions. Craft determines whether those conditions
become a living story.

### 2.5 Reading band and interest age are different decisions

Every manuscript brief MUST declare two separate fields:

- **Reading band:** the print, language, decoding, sentence, pattern, layout and
  inference demands placed on the child reader.
- **Interest age:** the likely fit of the topic, humour, stakes, emotional situation,
  knowledge and illustration treatment. For this library the intended interest range is
  usually within ages 4–8, but an individual child's interests and language experience
  take precedence.

Level A, B or C is never shorthand for a child's age. A seven-year-old beginning reader
may need Level A print about a topic with seven-year-old appeal. A four-year-old may enjoy
a more conceptually rich book read aloud. Do not lower the interest, humour or dignity of
a book merely because its print is highly controlled.

LiteracyPath's A–C labels are internal, Fountas-and-Pinnell-style early-emergent bands,
not official Fountas & Pinnell certification and not interchangeable with a publisher's
“Level 1.” Placement considers the whole text: genre and form, structure, content,
themes and ideas, language and literary features, sentence complexity, vocabulary, word
demands, illustrations, and book and print features. The hard product limits in section
5 still apply.

| Band | Reading work | Writer's priority |
|---|---|---|
| A | Highly controlled, short connected text; strong picture support; familiar syntax; an easy-to-hear pattern | Confidence, concrete action, obvious reference and a complete micro-meaning across pages |
| B | A little more print and pattern variation; broader high-frequency vocabulary; simple causal or concept links; brief dialogue where permitted | Fluency through meaningful repetition, clear reaction and consequence, and a visible turn or synthesis |
| C | Still an early-emergent band; more varied sentence shapes, punctuation, vocabulary, dialogue and inference, with supportive illustrations | A fuller causal or informational sequence without drifting into adult summary, textbook register or unnecessary density |

Interest age shapes the choice and treatment of material:

| Interest consideration | Editorial question |
|---|---|
| Familiar experience | Is there an immediate child-sized point of entry without assuming one universal family, home or routine? |
| Curiosity and knowledge | Is the subject genuinely worth discovering, including for an older beginning reader? |
| Humour and tension | Does the event feel funny, surprising, worrying or satisfying at the intended emotional distance? |
| Dignity | Would a child near the top of the interest range feel respected rather than talked down to? |
| Support | Can illustration, adult read-aloud and discussion carry conceptual richness without making the printed line harder to read? |

Difficulty must come from intentionally richer meaning, language or structure—not from
vagueness, cramped layout, unexplained jumps or obscure wording.

## 3. Story architecture

### 3.1 The one-sentence spine

Before drafting fiction pages, write:

> When **[initiating event]**, **[protagonist]** wants **[concrete goal]**, but
> **[obstacle]**, so they **[attempt]** and finally **[decisive action]**, leaving
> **[changed outcome]**.

If this sentence cannot be completed without “and then” more than once, the plot is likely
a list of events rather than one story.

Before drafting nonfiction pages, write:

> The reader starts with **[question or observation]**; each page develops it through
> **[one organising relationship]**; the final page leaves the reader understanding
> **[answer, pattern or significance]**.

If the facts can be freely shuffled without weakening the book, the concept spine is not
yet strong enough.

### 3.2 Required fiction beats

1. **Orientation:** who, where, and the normal state.
2. **Initiating event:** something changes.
3. **Goal:** the protagonist’s want becomes concrete.
4. **First attempt:** sensible but incomplete.
5. **Consequence:** the world changes because of the attempt.
6. **Escalation:** the next attempt is harder, different or more costly.
7. **Genuine failure or reversal:** an action fails and costs at least one story beat.
8. **Turning point:** the protagonist notices, chooses or combines what has been learned.
9. **Climax:** the decisive attempt is shown.
10. **Resolution:** the original goal is visibly resolved, altered or deliberately abandoned.
11. **Landing:** one image or line lets the child feel the new state.

These functions may be compressed or combined to fit the reading band. Level A uses the
micro-arc below rather than eleven separately labelled pages.

### 3.3 The Level A micro-arc

A Level A book is small, not unfinished. It does not need eleven separately named beats,
but it MUST contain a complete change the child can track:

1. **Starting state:** a character, place, object or condition is clear.
2. **Change or want:** something is needed, missing, difficult, surprising or about to
   cause an effect.
3. **Action and progression:** the character tries, repeats with meaningful change, or
   meets an escalating result.
4. **Turn:** somebody notices, stops, chooses, responds or tries differently.
5. **Outcome and landing:** the opening change is answered and the final page shows what
   is now different.

Several functions may share one page, and the illustration may carry emotion or spatial
information, but the printed text must state any action, reaction or consequence needed
to understand the ending. A row of similar actions followed by “they go,” “she moves,”
“he is done” or another stop-state is not a micro-arc.

For patterned Level A fiction, use this movement:

> establish the pattern → change its meaning → create an effect → break or reframe the
> pattern → land on the changed state

For patterned Level A nonfiction, use:

> establish the organising question or category → add related examples in an
> intelligible order → reveal the relationship → answer or reframe the opening

The protagonist need not solve everything alone, but their noticing, choice, stopping,
asking, helping or new attempt must matter to the outcome.

### 3.4 Social consequence, boundary and repair

When a character repeatedly annoys, frightens, excludes, soaks, bumps, breaks, scatters
or damages, that behavior becomes part of the story's central causal chain. The book
MUST NOT leave the social meaning only in irritated faces in the last illustration.

The route must show:

1. **Impact:** what happened to the other character or their object.
2. **Readable reaction:** a concrete action, distance, posture or short line that makes
   the response legible. The narrator need not label the feeling.
3. **Recognition:** the instigator notices the effect, or another character names a clear
   boundary.
4. **Consequence or boundary:** the behavior changes what may happen next.
5. **Proportionate repair:** clean, return, rebuild, help, apologize in
   character-specific words, offer space, or try again differently.
6. **Payoff:** the final beat shows the repaired object, safer play, changed action or
   relationship.

The language can remain very short. “Grumpy drops the pail” gives more usable story
information than “Grumpy is annoyed.” “Splashy fetches the cloth” earns more than
“Splashy learns to be kind.”

Do not:

- make hurt or damage disappear because everyone laughs;
- punish or humiliate a character to manufacture a lesson;
- let an adult solve the whole problem while the protagonist watches;
- replace repair with a promise about next time;
- demand instant forgiveness from the affected character;
- state a moral after the action has already shown it.

A mischievous character may remain mischievous. What must change is their understanding
of this effect, this boundary or this repair. The ending should preserve personality
while resolving the immediate harm.

Editorial test: **If the affected character were removed, would the ending read the
same?** If yes, the social consequence has not been written into the story.

### 3.5 Cause-and-effect rule

For every page after the first, the editor must complete:

> This happens **because** ______ on the previous page or route state.

“Because the author needed another choice” fails.

### 3.6 The concrete spine object

Use one recurring object, place, creature or visible condition as the story spine:

- missing hat;
- map;
- bell;
- lunch basket;
- fallen stones;
- purple potion;
- lost frog;
- hidden glow.

The spine should appear in text or art often enough that a young reader can track it. Do
not replace it halfway through with a new puzzle.

### 3.7 Earned endings

An ending is the consequence of the story, not merely its final location. The last page
MUST answer the opening want, problem, need or question and let the child feel the new
state.

An earned fiction ending may use one or more of these forms:

- **Goal payoff:** the wanted thing is found, made, reached or deliberately let go.
- **Repair:** damage, exclusion or upset receives a visible, proportionate response.
- **Changed action:** the character behaves differently in the same kind of moment.
- **Callback:** an opening line, image, object or refrain returns with new meaning.
- **Reversal or joke:** the resolved situation produces a final, causally prepared
  surprise.
- **Quiet landing:** a concrete sound, gesture, image or brief line allows the new state
  to settle.

The following do not resolve a story by themselves:

- leaving through a gate, walking away, going home or going to sleep;
- “the plan works,” “the team is ready” or a list of completed jobs;
- an abstract statement about memories, friendship, safety or teamwork;
- a promise to behave differently tomorrow;
- a last-page summary of the lesson;
- a fresh problem introduced after the main goal has closed;
- an image that shows the reaction or resolution when the essential action is absent
  from the text.

Movement can be an ending only when the movement itself fulfils the established goal or
demonstrates the earned change. Sleep can be an ending only when returning to sleep
resolves the established problem. A deliberately open ending must still close the
immediate story question.

Use the **empty-exit test**:

1. Remove the last page. If the story means exactly the same thing, that page is empty.
2. Ask what is now different from the opening.
3. Point to the action or recognition that caused the difference.
4. Check that the affected characters, goal object and relevant prop state appear in the
   resolution.
5. Read the last two pages aloud. The final line should land, not sound like the next
   sentence was accidentally deleted.

## 4. Writing meaningful Story Quest branches

### 4.1 A real choice

A choice is meaningful when:

- both options are plausible for that character;
- the verbs differ;
- the next scene differs;
- the consequence is visible;
- the story remembers the choice for at least one full scene;
- neither option is merely “be good” versus “be bad”;
- the story does not overrule the child on the next page.

Good:

- “Follow the tracks” / “Listen by the reeds”
- “Wash the hat” / “Fan the hat”
- “Let Fern sing” / “Check the green page”

Weak:

- “Keep going” / “Try again”
- “Be kind” / “Be mean”
- “Look here” / “Look there” when both immediately show the same image
- two buttons that lead to the same next scene

### 4.2 Choice language

- Two options per scene.
- Four words or fewer per option.
- Start with a concrete verb where possible.
- Name the object or character when ambiguity is possible.
- Never depend on a child remembering which of two identical-looking characters is meant.
- Avoid repeated generic prompts such as “What happens now?” Use a prompt tied to the
  current goal: “How can they cross?” or “Where should Chompy look?”
- Narrate the prompt if the child is expected to act on it.

### 4.3 Branch state ledger

Every quest requires a state table before prose:

| State | Allowed values | Changed by | Must remain visible until |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goal object | lost / found / dirty / clean | named choice and scene | resolution |
| Companion | Pip / Stone / both | choice | next explicit reunion |
| Prop | carried / dropped / broken | shown action | shown repair or abandonment |
| Location | barn / pond / tree | travel scene | next travel scene |
| Relationship | upset / repairing / restored | consequence | responsive action |

No route may:

- show a recovered item as still missing;
- restore a broken object without a repair scene;
- introduce a companion who was not called or met;
- change weather, time of day or location without a transition;
- use knowledge obtained only on another branch;
- claim an apology or repair happened off-screen.

### 4.4 Merges

Branches may merge to control scope, but:

- not on the immediate next scene;
- not before the chosen action has a visible consequence;
- not if merging erases a broken/found/dirty/carrying state;
- not if the merged text describes only one of the incoming routes.

At a merge, write and test the text against **every incoming state**.

### 4.5 Endings

Every Story Quest ending MUST pass section 3.7 and MUST:

- resolve or consciously change the opening goal;
- include the protagonist and every route state needed to understand the outcome;
- show the result in the image;
- preserve the visible consequence of the route's choices;
- differ materially from other endings;
- avoid a stated moral;
- offer “Read again” and “Finish.”

Changing only the final adjective does not make a new ending.

## 5. Reading bands

Reading band controls what the child must do with the printed text. Interest age controls
what the book is about and how it treats the reader. They must be declared separately.
Placement is based on the complete manuscript and layout, not on age or word count alone.

All child-facing titles, narration, labels, prompts and choices use U.S. English spelling.
Research quotations keep their source spelling and are never copied into manuscripts.

These are LiteracyPath's internal early-emergent profiles. They draw on published A–C
descriptors but retain the product's existing hard page, line and word limits.

### 5.1 Level A

**Reader:** beginning reader working in highly controlled connected text
**Purpose:** confidence, one clear micro-meaning, strong image support, familiar syntax
and an easy-to-hear pattern with meaningful change

Hard limits:

- 1 line per page;
- 6 words per line;
- 6 words per page;
- 5–10 scenes on a route;
- no dialogue;
- no subordinate clauses;
- no semicolon, colon, dash or internal comma;
- no abstract moral language;
- no more than 30% multisyllabic word types;
- choice labels no longer than 4 words.

Writing rules:

- Use present tense.
- Prefer subject–verb–object.
- Repeat a useful sentence frame with one meaningful changing element.
- Keep pronoun reference obvious; repeat a name if needed.
- Use concrete nouns and visible verbs.
- One goal, one main problem, one primary prop.
- Art may clarify the event but must not be required to guess an untaught word.
- Build a complete micro-arc or concept progression across the pages; a repeated frame is
  a carrier of meaning, not the plot itself.
- Put any action, social effect or turn required for comprehension in the text, even when
  the illustration also shows it.

Example frame:

> The hat is in the mud.

Not Level A:

> Muddy wondered whether the hat might be somewhere behind the old barn.

### 5.2 Level B

**Reader:** early reader ready for a little more print, variation and causal or concept
connection
**Purpose:** fluent connected reading, meaningful pattern variation, brief
character-specific dialogue where permitted, visible consequence and repair

Hard limits:

- up to 2 lines per page;
- up to 8 words per line;
- up to 14 words per page;
- 6–12 scenes on a route;
- dialogue on no more than half of pages;
- no subordinate clauses in the current product standard;
- no more than 35% multisyllabic word types;
- choice labels no longer than 4 words.

Writing rules:

- Use present tense narration.
- Allow short, character-specific dialogue.
- Maintain one main goal and at most one supporting goal.
- A mistake should produce a consequence and a repair.
- Use conjunctions sparingly and only where the sentence remains easy to parse.
- New vocabulary must be inferable from action, briefly supported in audio or revisited.
- Let repeated frames evolve with the action; do not fill the middle by swapping nouns
  into an unchanged sentence.
- Show reaction and repair in concrete action before adding dialogue or explanation.

Example:

> Chompy sees the full basket.
> “Lunch for all,” says Sunny.

### 5.3 Level C

**Reader:** early reader ready for more varied syntax, vocabulary, punctuation, dialogue
and supported inference
**Purpose:** a fuller causal, mystery or informational sequence with distinctive voice
and precise detail

Hard limits:

- standard Level C books: up to 3 lines and 22 words per page;
- Moonwood extended-narrative books: one connected paragraph designed to wrap to
  roughly 4–5 short reader lines, normally 24–34 words and never more than 38 words;
- Moonwood paragraphs use 2–4 short sentences, with no sentence carrying more than
  one main action plus its immediate cause, reaction or result;
- 8–14 scenes on a route;
- no more than 40% multisyllabic word types;
- choice labels no longer than 4 words.

Writing rules:

- Use present tense narration in Story Quests for consistency.
- Allow concise dialogue with distinct voices.
- Permit one main goal and clearly subordinate steps.
- Ask the reader to infer motive or cause from staged evidence, not missing information.
- Seed every magical rule before it resolves the plot.
- Track objects, clues and knowledge across branches.
- Use vivid words, but only when they do precise work.
- More room is permission for richer meaning, not for report language, abstract
  conclusions or padded explanation.
- Keep inference fair: stage the clue, motive, reaction or concept relationship before
  asking the child to connect it.
- In the Moonwood extended-narrative profile, name the acting character, object and
  consequence clearly enough that a child never has to decode a cryptic fragment or guess
  what an unexplained pronoun refers to.
- Each Moonwood page must make sense as a paragraph and must also pick up a visible action,
  object, question or feeling from the previous page before preparing the next beat.

Example:

> Gold light leaks under the stone.
> Dewdrop presses one ear to the water.
> “It is awake,” she says.

Moonwood extended-narrative example:

> Gold light leaks from beneath the stone, so Dewdrop kneels beside the stream.
> She presses one ear to the water and hears a slow humming sound.
> “Something under there is awake,” she tells Pip.

### 5.4 Early decodable track

“Early” is a phonics track, not simply an easier Level A.

- Declare the exact grapheme–phoneme correspondences available.
- Declare common exception words.
- Every word must be decodable from that scope or explicitly taught as an exception.
- Do not add an attractive but out-of-scope destination word.
- Do not make pictures a guessing strategy.
- Keep the story coherent despite the restricted code.

## 6. Nonfiction architecture for ages 4–8

Nonfiction does not need a fictional obstacle or character change. It does need a reason
to turn the page, a coherent concept spine and a real close.

### 6.1 Start with an inquiry or organising idea

Open with one of:

- a concrete question the pages will answer;
- an observable surprise;
- a whole that will be examined in parts;
- a change the reader can follow;
- a comparison the reader can test;
- a place, object or living thing viewed more closely.

Do not open by announcing a school topic in generic language. “Here are facts about
rocks” supplies no reading purpose.

### 6.2 Choose one concept progression

Build the book around one main structure:

- time or life-cycle sequence;
- cause and effect;
- whole to parts;
- category and meaningful examples;
- compare and contrast;
- habitat or location journey;
- problem and response;
- observation to explanation;
- scale, number or distance.

Every page must add, refine, contrast or connect information. A list is acceptable only
when its order or grouping helps the child discover a relationship. If the pages can be
shuffled without losing meaning, strengthen the concept structure.

### 6.3 Write facts in a human child-facing voice

- Prefer precise nouns and active verbs to broad praise or textbook labels.
- Start with what the child can see, then name or explain it.
- Put new vocabulary beside an image, action, example or plain-language explanation that
  makes it usable.
- Use comparisons grounded in familiar scale without pretending every child shares one
  home, culture or experience.
- Keep referents exact. “It,” “they” and “this” must point to one obvious thing.
- Vary syntax and page openings within the band's limits.
- Let sound, surprise, contrast, pattern and direct address create warmth where
  appropriate, without inventing facts or making every animal speak.
- Qualify claims honestly. Avoid “all,” “always,” “never” and “only” unless the evidence
  supports them.
- Check every factual claim and every image; attractive art does not excuse a false
  label, scale, habitat, sequence or behavior.

Avoid:

- one disconnected specimen or fact per page;
- encyclopedia compression;
- definition chains with no observable meaning;
- “Scientists study this” as a generic ending;
- “X is amazing,” “X is everywhere” or “There is so much to learn” as closure;
- safety or behavior advice that replaces the promised science;
- anthropomorphism that makes a factual action untrue.

### 6.4 Close the inquiry

An earned nonfiction ending may:

- answer the opening question;
- show the completed change or cycle;
- return to the opening image with new understanding;
- reveal the relationship among the examples;
- make a final, supported comparison;
- move from a specific observation to its larger meaning;
- invite one concrete act of looking, noticing or checking that follows from the book's
  information.

The final page must add synthesis, not another unrelated fact. The child should be able
to explain why that page comes last.

### 6.5 Reading band versus subject

Ages 4–8 may share interest in animals, weather, machines, bodies, space, habitats,
materials, communities and change. Do not assign those subjects to a higher or lower
band by topic alone. Keep the concept dignified and control the printed language, layout
and image support for the declared band.

Level A nonfiction still needs connected meaning. Level B should make a simple
relationship visible. Level C may carry a fuller explanation or comparison, but it must
remain early-emergent text rather than a miniature adult article.

## 7. Human writing and the anti-slop standard

### 7.1 Signs of generated or generic prose

Reject or rewrite:

- “Everyone smiled.”
- “They learned that…”
- “From that day on…”
- “It was a magical adventure.”
- “Together they knew anything was possible.”
- “A warm feeling filled…”
- “His frown softened.”
- “She almost smiled.”
- “They carefully made sure everyone was safe.”
- “Their new team works.”
- “The plan is complete.”
- “They move beyond the gate.”
- repeated “What happens next?” prompts;
- three near-synonymous adjectives;
- a sudden group celebration after a trivial action;
- a character cameo that has no want or causal role;
- an object with a face, wink, smile or personality for no established reason;
- purple glow, sparkles or “ancient magic” used only to create atmosphere;
- an apology, friendship speech or moral replacing a concrete repair;
- conflict announced and resolved on the same page;
- characters explaining feelings the picture and action should show;
- adults’ therapy or risk-assessment language in a child’s mouth;
- perfectly polite characters who never make mistakes;
- arbitrary alliteration that distorts natural speech;
- “and then” plotting with no causal chain.

### 7.2 Human-specificity test

Each fiction story needs details that could belong only to this character and world:

- Grumpy quietly empties a basket instead of giving a friendship speech.
- Stone’s huge call makes a small frog hide.
- Speedy outruns the map and must recover it.
- Muddy finds a clean hat and creates a mud problem.

If the names can be swapped with characters from another world without changing the plot,
the story is generic.

Each nonfiction book needs equally particular details: this animal's movement, this
material's behavior, this stage of change, this measurable contrast. If the topic noun
can be swapped without rebuilding the sentences and progression, the book is a template,
not an inquiry.

### 7.3 Emotion rule

Stage emotion through:

- action;
- distance;
- posture;
- gaze;
- what a character protects, drops, avoids or repairs;
- one short line of dialogue.

Do not diagnose the emotion for the child when the scene can show it.

### 7.4 Moral rule

The character may change behavior. The narrator may not state the lesson.

Good:

> Flint shuts the lantern. He listens.

Weak:

> Flint learned that listening is always better than rushing.

### 7.5 Spoken syntax and page rhythm

Children's-book prose is written for a mouth, an ear and a page turn as well as for the
eye.

- Write in breath-sized phrases within the band limits.
- Put the important concrete word where natural spoken stress can carry it.
- Prefer ordinary spoken order unless a refrain has earned a deliberate inversion.
- Use strong verbs before adding adverbs or adjective stacks.
- Repeat names when reference would otherwise blur, but vary line shape so the prose does
  not become a register of names and actions.
- Use contractions and fragments only when they sound like real speech and fit the
  declared reading or phonics scope.
- Use sound words, interjections and punctuation only when they represent a real event or
  response. They are not decoration.
- Let a page turn conceal a reveal, consequence, pattern break or answer when the layout
  allows it.
- Alternate tension and release. Even very short prose should not give every line
  identical weight and cadence.

Read the text aloud exactly as printed. Do not silently add missing function words,
repair the grammar or change the emphasis to make a weak line work.

### 7.6 Patterned language must move

Pattern gives a beginning reader confidence and anticipation. Variation gives the
pattern a reason to continue.

A repeated frame must do at least one of these on each return:

- advance action;
- increase or reduce a problem;
- change place, size, number, sound, state or point of view;
- reveal a reaction;
- add a connected fact;
- prepare a turn, joke, answer or callback.

The pattern should become audible before it becomes flat. At the decisive moment, break
it, shorten it, reverse it or return it with changed meaning. Do not preserve a sentence
frame so rigidly that characters speak unnaturally.

Editorial deletion test: remove one repeated page. If the story, emotion or concept
sequence does not change, the page is filler.

### 7.7 Report language versus book language

Report language certifies that something happened without giving the child the event:
“the team works,” “everyone is ready,” “the plan is complete,” “they move beyond the
gate,” or “they make sure it is safe.”

Replace the report with the action, image, sound, comparison or character line that
proves it. The precise replacement depends on the manuscript; a stock “happy” line is
not an improvement.

Short does not mean robotic. Use familiar connective tissue when it is needed for
meaning, and cut it when page sequence or image already carries the link. Choose the
smallest natural sentence, not the smallest possible collection of words.

### 7.8 Natural voice edit

Readable is not enough. A sentence can be grammatical and still sound as if it came from
a report, a lesson plan or a text generator. Every manuscript therefore needs a separate
natural-voice edit after its reading-band edit.

Use these rules:

- Put the thing on the page. Prefer a warm windowsill, a muddy paw or a bent red hat to
  *warmth*, *curiosity*, *teamwork* or another abstract label.
- Let behavior reveal character. Do not convert several funny actions into a neat list of
  personality traits.
- Use the words that character would actually choose. Child dialogue is usually shorter,
  less formal and less complete than narration. Use contractions when the reading band
  allows them.
- Give different characters different rhythms, favorite words and ways of avoiding a
  question. If a line can move to another character unchanged, rewrite it.
- Vary sentence length and openings within the level limits. Reject a run of sentences with
  the same shape, repeated three-part lists or a metronomic subject-verb-object rhythm.
- Remove empty intensifiers, scene summaries and explanations that add no new picture or
  action. Strong nouns and verbs should carry the sentence.
- Keep one small surprise, joke, awkward pause or imperfect response when it belongs to the
  character. Do not polish every exchange into equally helpful, equally sensible speech.
- End on an action, image or line of dialogue when possible. Do not add a tidy optimistic
  conclusion to explain what the child should have understood.
- Read every page sequence aloud twice. Rewrite any tongue-catch, unnatural emphasis,
  repeated cadence or line that no adult would comfortably say to a child.

Reject:

> They learned he loved warmth, shiny objects and interesting work.

That describes a puppy like an employee appraisal. Show the puppy or let a person speak:

> “Hide anything shiny,” Uncle Eddie said, “and shut the airing cupboard.”

The second line belongs to this dog, this family and this moment. It also gives the
illustrator and the child something concrete to picture.

Editorial test: remove the character names and world nouns. If the sentence still reads like
a generic summary that could fit almost any story, it has not passed.

Research basis for this standard:

- George Mason University's Writing Center recommends saying exactly what is meant,
  deleting words without lost meaning, using concrete examples and reading prose aloud.
- Texas A&M's Writing Center recommends reading aloud and varying sentence lengths, types
  and openings to improve rhythm and flow.
- Expert readers in the ACL 2025 study *What Makes AI-Generated Text Detectable?* repeatedly
  identified predictable structure, repetitive or overly formal wording, homogeneous voices,
  over-explanation and overly tidy positive conclusions as signs of generated prose.

Sources:

- https://writingcenter.gmu.edu/writing-resources/general-writing-practices/writing-concisely
- https://writingcenter.tamu.edu/guides/resources/flow.html
- https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.267/

## 8. Story creation and revision workflow

### Revision triage: light, moderate or structural

Classify an existing book only after reading the complete text in page order and checking
every illustration. The class describes the deepest problem, not how many words change.

**Light edit**

Use when the concept or story spine, page order, image meaning and ending already work.
Revise line-level wording, spoken rhythm, reference, band fit and landing without
changing what a page depicts.

**Moderate edit**

Use when the identity and broad spine are worth keeping, but several pages need new
wording or re-sequencing to strengthen agency, causality, pattern progression, concept
structure, social response or closure. Confirm that every revised page still tells the
truth about its existing illustration.

**Structural edit**

Use when the book lacks an on-page goal or organising inquiry, replaces causality with
incidents, leaves harm or annoyance unresolved, ends with an empty exit, postpones the
central goal, contradicts its own state, or requires a different beat order. Rebuild the
spine before polishing sentences. Structural work may require new or changed art.

Visual inspection may raise or lower the class. Do not force a structural manuscript
through a light edit merely to preserve existing audio or image approvals.

After any text edit:

- freeze page order and text before rebuilding dependent media;
- re-check every changed line against its image;
- regenerate exact-text narration for every changed page and affected isolated word;
- refresh any attached teacher discussion prompt whose answer or evidence changed;
- refresh text, visual and audio fingerprints only after their reviews pass.

### Gate 1 — Canon

- Select reading band and interest age separately.
- Select world and canonical character IDs where they apply.
- Attach model sheets and scale lineup where characters recur.
- State each fiction character’s want and behavioral engine, or the nonfiction inquiry
  and concept progression.
- Resolve any existing visual conflict before drafting.

### Gate 2 — Narrative or concept spine

- For fiction, write the one-sentence narrative spine.
- Name the initiating event, goal, obstacle, attempts, genuine failure, turning point,
  climax and outcome at the scale the reading band permits.
- Name the concrete spine object or condition.
- Prove this plot could not be reassigned unchanged to another cast.
- For nonfiction, write the inquiry spine, choose one organising relationship and state
  what the last page will help the child understand.
- Prove the facts form a progression rather than a shuffleable list.

### Gate 3 — Branch map

- Draw the complete page graph.
- Add the state ledger.
- List every ending.
- Check every path reaches an ending.
- Check every choice edge and every merge against all incoming states.
- Keep all route lengths inside the level stamina limit.

### Gate 4 — Text

- Draft to the band limits from the first sentence.
- Remove explanation, hedging, generic emotion and repeated prompts.
- Confirm each page follows because of the previous scene.
- Confirm every declared target word appears.
- Read every page and route aloud exactly as printed without silently correcting it.
- Mark tongue-catches, accidental rhyme, unnatural stress, repeated cadence and ambiguous
  pronouns.
- Listen across page turns for rhythm, anticipation and landing.
- Compare dialogue aloud so character voices do not collapse into one polite adult voice.
- For patterned books, identify where anticipation becomes boredom or guessing replaces
  reading.

### Gate 5 — Art

- Create or attach the locked sequence sheet.
- Make page art from the final text and state ledger.
- Compare adjacent pages at full size.
- Compare every character against the master, not just the previous generated page.
- Reject wrong species, size, color, clothing, prop state, weather or style.

### Gate 6 — Audio

- Freeze text.
- Generate word-for-word narration.
- Run file, text-hash, pronunciation, pacing and intelligibility checks.

### Gate 7 — Child read and read-aloud playtest

Use the book in the way its declared band and interest age intend: independent reading,
supported reading or adult read-aloud. Do not explain the plot or concept before the
first pass and do not rescue an unclear manuscript with leading questions.

Observe:

- where the child hesitates, guesses from the pattern or picture, rereads or loses the
  referent;
- whether the child anticipates page turns, laughs, worries, comments or asks to
  continue;
- whether repeated language supports fluency or becomes mechanical;
- whether the child notices action, reaction, cause and changed state;
- whether the last page feels like an ending to the child.

For fiction, ask in open language:

- Who is the story about?
- What did they want or need?
- What changed or went wrong?
- What did they try?
- What happened because of that?
- How was it different at the end?

When behavior affects another character, also ask:

- What happened to the other character?
- How could you tell?
- What did the first character do next?

For nonfiction, ask:

- What was the book helping you find out?
- How did the pages fit together?
- What is one thing you can explain now?
- Why did this page come last?

For a Story Quest, ask what the child's choice changed.

Observe rather than coach. Misreading, pattern-guessing, boredom, confused causality, an
invisible social reaction or failure to recognize the ending is a manuscript signal.
Revise the text, concept sequence or image before blaming the reader.

### Gate 8 — Adult review

A teacher/editor checks:

- reading band and interest age separately;
- decodability where claimed;
- vocabulary;
- cause and effect or nonfiction concept progression;
- nonfiction facts, scope and sources;
- ending, social consequence and repair;
- exact read-aloud findings;
- world and character canon;
- accessibility;
- image–text match;
- audio match;
- cultural and social representation;
- replay value.

## 9. Editorial scorecard

Score each category from 0–4.

| Category | 0 | 2 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Character / subject | interchangeable or generic | recognisable but thin | unmistakably this character, world or subject |
| Goal / inquiry | absent | present but vague | early, concrete and sustained |
| Causality / concept progression | incidents or shuffled facts | mostly linked | every beat or fact earns the next and the child can explain the link |
| Obstacle / development | none | mild delay or loose sequence | meaningful failed attempt and escalation, or revealing concept development |
| Choices / page-turn purpose | cosmetic or filler | some consequence or anticipation | visible, remembered consequence, or a compelling reveal, contrast or answer |
| Ending | stops, exits, postpones or states a lesson | closes the topic or plot but lands weakly | pays off the opening through changed state, repair, callback, synthesis or a prepared final beat |
| Language band | age label or word count substitutes for placement | technically inside caps | holistic band fit with dignified interest, natural syntax and clear image support |
| Read-aloud voice | robotic, generic or report-like | competent | speakable, varied, specific and memorable within the band |
| Visual continuity | contradictory | mostly consistent | model-perfect across every scene |
| Image–text match | conflicting | generally aligned | every image advances comprehension |
| Audio | absent/stale | present | exact, clear and fully verified |
| Delight | none | pleasant | funny, tense, moving or wondrous enough to reread |

Release threshold:

- no category below 3;
- at least 38/48 overall;
- Character/subject, Causality/concept progression, Language band, Visual continuity,
  Image–text match and Audio must each score 4 for a flagship release.

## 10. Final preflight

Before marking a book “ready to ship,” answer **yes** to every applicable item. A failed
item cannot be relabelled “not applicable” to avoid revision.

### 10.1 Placement, language and delight

- [ ] Reading band and interest age are declared separately.
- [ ] Placement considers the whole text and layout, not age or word count alone.
- [ ] The subject and tone respect children across the intended interest range.
- [ ] The prose fits the exact Level A/B/C or declared decodable limits.
- [ ] The manuscript has passed an exact adult read-aloud without silent repair.
- [ ] Dialogue sounds like these characters, not generic “nice” characters.
- [ ] Every repeated line advances action, reaction, information or anticipation.
- [ ] The pattern changes, breaks or returns with new meaning at the turn or close.
- [ ] Repetition supports fluency without replacing decoding.
- [ ] Every target word is useful, present and supported.
- [ ] An editor would choose to read it again without the educational wrapper.

### 10.2 Fiction

- [ ] I can state the protagonist's goal in one concrete sentence.
- [ ] The opening creates the goal quickly.
- [ ] A Level A story has a complete micro-arc, not only a repeated frame.
- [ ] The protagonist causes events through choices and attempts.
- [ ] An attempt genuinely fails, complicates the goal or creates a meaningful effect at
      the scale of the band.
- [ ] Every scene follows causally from the page or route state before it.
- [ ] If behavior affects another character, the text makes impact and response legible.
- [ ] Recognition, boundary or proportionate repair resolves the immediate social effect.
- [ ] The story does not state its moral.
- [ ] The ending changes or answers the opening state on the page.
- [ ] The last page passes the empty-exit test.
- [ ] No essential reaction or resolution exists only in the illustration.
- [ ] A child can retell who wanted what, what changed and how it ended.

### 10.3 Nonfiction

- [ ] The book opens a concrete inquiry, observation or organising idea.
- [ ] The pages follow one coherent concept progression.
- [ ] The order teaches a relationship; the facts are not freely shuffleable.
- [ ] Every factual claim and image is supportable and accurately scoped.
- [ ] New vocabulary is made usable through image, action, example or explanation.
- [ ] The ending answers, synthesises, compares or meaningfully returns to the opening.
- [ ] A child can explain what the book helped them discover and why the final page comes
      last.

### 10.4 Story Quests

- [ ] Every choice has two plausible, distinct consequences.
- [ ] No merge erases prior state.
- [ ] Every ending resolves the opening goal and differs materially.
- [ ] The full route graph, every edge and every ending pass automated checks.
- [ ] A child can explain what their choice changed.

### 10.5 Canon, image and audio

- [ ] Every character uses a world-qualified canon ID where a recurring canon applies.
- [ ] Species, palette, markings, clothing and scale match the master.
- [ ] Weather, location, prop and relationship states remain continuous.
- [ ] Every image depicts the exact text and route state.
- [ ] No image contains malformed text, extra limbs, duplicate bodies or faceless living
      characters.
- [ ] Every page or scene has exact word-for-word narration.
- [ ] Changed text, teacher discussion prompts, images and audio have passed their current release gates.

If the final answer is not yes, the book is not finished.
