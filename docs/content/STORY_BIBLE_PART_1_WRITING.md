# Story Bible — Part I: How to Write an Excellent Children's Story

**Status:** Canonical writing and editorial standard
**Version:** 1.0
**Date:** 31 July 2026
**Applies to:** Guided-reading fiction, picture books, Story Quests and story-led games

This is the research-led craft standard. It explains how a children's story should work
before any Literacy Guide character, setting, image or audio is selected. A story that
breaks a **MUST** rule does not ship.

Part II contains the separate Literacy Guide world and character canon.

## 1. What every children's story must do

Every story MUST:

1. Give a recognisable character a concrete want, problem or question.
2. Make that goal clear near the beginning.
3. Let the character act. Events must not simply happen around them.
4. Link actions and consequences so a child can explain why the next event happened.
5. Include at least one meaningful obstacle, mistake, surprise or failed attempt.
6. Build toward a turning point where the character’s next action matters most.
7. Resolve the story’s central goal on the page, not off-screen.
8. End with a changed situation, not a stated lesson.
9. Use words, sentence length, story length and inference demands appropriate to its
   declared reading level.
10. Make the illustration carry real story information while still allowing the printed
    words to be decoded rather than guessed.
11. preserve the characters’ established personality, species, size, palette, clothing,
    relationships, speech style and world.
12. Give every Story Quest choice a visible and logically different consequence.
13. Supply word-for-word matching narration for every page or scene.
14. Be enjoyable as a story even if every teaching label, reward and progress counter is
    removed.

The test is simple: a child should be able to say **who wanted what, what got in the way,
what they tried, what happened because of it, and how it ended**.

## 2. The evidence behind the standard

These rules combine children’s-literature craft with evidence about how young children
understand and learn from narrative.

### 2.1 Proven principles

- **Goal, attempt and outcome are the backbone of comprehension.** Research on narrative
  comprehension identifies a character’s goal, attempts and outcomes as fundamental.
  Logically matched goal structures make stories easier to understand. An initiating event
  should create the story problem and the main goal should drive what follows.
- **Keep the goal object present.** Repeated visual or verbal presence of the thing a
  character wants helps children connect the goal to later attempts and consequences.
- **Strong narrative and emotional engagement matter.** The Department for Education
  recommends stories that elicit curiosity, excitement, laughter or empathy; sustain repeat
  readings; extend vocabulary; and use engaging, inclusive illustrations.
- **Re-reading adds depth.** Familiarity increases emotional engagement, supports retelling
  and gives children another encounter with language and character.
- **Connected text matters.** The US What Works Clearinghouse recommends that children read
  connected text every day to develop accuracy, fluency and comprehension.
- **Decodable means decodable.** Text for a beginning reader must match the grapheme–phoneme
  correspondences they have learned. Pictures must enrich meaning, not make the child guess
  an undecodable word.
- **Oral language supports literacy.** The Education Endowment Foundation finds strong
  evidence for purposeful speaking and listening, especially book discussion, vocabulary
  work and structured questions integrated with story reading.
- **Choice and interest support engagement.** National Literacy Trust guidance reports that
  agency and choice can increase motivation, effort and persistence in reading.
- **Pictures are narrative, not decoration.** Research using wordless picture books shows
  that young children build and demonstrate narrative comprehension from a coherent visual
  sequence. Every image therefore has to preserve cause, state and continuity.
- **A problem makes a story; a list of events does not.** Children’s author Joyce Dunbar’s
  BookTrust guidance stresses a problem, an obstacle, a turning point and a resolution.
  Every word must earn its place.

### 2.3 Evidence-to-practice matrix

| Evidence or expert principle | What it means for a writer | Mandatory editorial test |
|---|---|---|
| Children understand a narrative through linked goals, attempts and outcomes | Establish a concrete want, let the protagonist try, and show what each attempt causes | Can a child point to the goal, an attempt and its outcome? |
| A visible goal object supports comprehension | Keep the missing, wanted or changing thing present in words or pictures | Does the story’s spine disappear for more than one beat without a reason? |
| Strong narrative invites emotional engagement and rereading | Build curiosity, humour, tension, empathy or wonder into the action itself | Would the story still be enjoyable without rewards or teaching labels? |
| Repeated readings deepen familiarity, retelling and language learning | Plant details, refrains and visual jokes that reward a second reading | Is there anything satisfying to notice or anticipate on rereading? |
| Connected text develops accuracy, fluency and comprehension | Let the child read meaningful sentences and stories, not only isolated drills | Does the educational target live inside coherent meaning? |
| Decodable text must match taught sound–letter knowledge | Control the code before adding “interesting” but inaccessible vocabulary | Can the intended reader decode every required word using taught knowledge? |
| Oral-language work is strongest when purposeful and integrated | Ask questions that help children predict, explain, compare or retell | Does a prompt deepen the story rather than interrupt it? |
| Agency can support motivation and persistence | Offer choices that affect action and consequence | Can the child describe what changed because of their choice? |
| Illustrations carry narrative information | Use images to show state, space, emotion, consequence and secondary action | Could the images be placed in the right order without reading the captions? |
| Picture-book language must be economical | Remove explanation that the action or image already communicates | Does every line advance action, character, mood, rhythm or comprehension? |

### 2.4 What the evidence does not justify

Research must never be used to excuse weak writing. It does **not** mean:

- every story needs a question after every page;
- every emotion should be named;
- repetition may replace plot progression;
- a moral should be stated at the end;
- “educational” vocabulary may exceed the declared decoding level;
- a colourful picture can rescue incoherent causality;
- two buttons constitute meaningful agency;
- adult approval is a substitute for a child understanding or enjoying the story.

The evidence determines the learning conditions. Craft determines whether those conditions
become a living story.

### 2.5 Developmental writing facts by reader age

These are design boundaries, not claims that all children of one age are identical.
Reading age, language experience, decoding knowledge and interest must still be considered.

| Intended reader | Writer must prioritise | Writer must avoid |
|---|---|---|
| 4–6 / emergent or low-level reader | one visible goal, short causal sequence, concrete verbs, familiar syntax, large visual changes, safe repetition, immediate choice consequences | hidden motives, long explanations, several unseen objects, time jumps, idioms needed to understand the plot |
| 5–7 / low-to-mid reader | clearer setbacks, simple inference from action and image, varied sentence openings, dialogue with speaker clarity, consequences lasting several beats | branches remembered only through abstract text, crowded casts, unseeded solutions, vocabulary density that stalls fluency |
| 7–9 / mid-to-high reader | layered motives, delayed payoff, stronger inference, subplots that support the main goal, more distinctive voice and rhythm | adult cynicism, arbitrary twists, unresolved main goals, complexity created only by longer sentences |

Difficulty must come from richer meaning, inference and structure—not from vagueness,
smaller type, longer instructions or obscure distractors.

## 3. Story architecture

### 3.1 The one-sentence spine

Before drafting pages, write:

> When **[initiating event]**, **[protagonist]** wants **[concrete goal]**, but
> **[obstacle]**, so they **[attempt]** and finally **[decisive action]**, leaving
> **[changed outcome]**.

If this sentence cannot be completed without “and then” more than once, the plot is likely
a list of events rather than one story.

### 3.2 Required beats

1. **Orientation:** who, where, and the normal state.
2. **Initiating event:** something changes.
3. **Goal:** the protagonist’s want becomes concrete.
4. **First attempt:** sensible but incomplete.
5. **Consequence:** the world changes because of the attempt.
6. **Escalation:** the next attempt is harder, different or more costly.
7. **Genuine failure or reversal:** an action fails and costs at least one story beat.
8. **Turning point:** the protagonist notices, chooses or combines what has been learned.
9. **Climax:** the decisive attempt is shown.
10. **Resolution:** the original goal is visibly resolved, altered or deliberately abandoned.
11. **Landing:** one image or line lets the child feel the new state.

Not every Level A text needs eleven separate pages, but every function must exist.

### 3.3 Cause-and-effect rule

For every page after the first, the editor must complete:

> This happens **because** ______ on the previous route.

“Because the author needed another choice” fails.

### 3.4 The concrete spine object

Use one recurring object, place, creature or visible condition as the story spine:

- missing hat;
- map;
- bell;
- lunch basket;
- fallen stones;
- purple potion;
- lost frog;
- hidden glow.

The spine should appear in text or art often enough that a young reader can track it. Do
not replace it halfway through with a new puzzle.

## 4. Writing meaningful Story Quest branches

### 4.1 A real choice

A choice is meaningful when:

- both options are plausible for that character;
- the verbs differ;
- the next scene differs;
- the consequence is visible;
- the story remembers the choice for at least one full scene;
- neither option is merely “be good” versus “be bad”;
- the story does not overrule the child on the next page.

Good:

- “Follow the tracks” / “Listen by the reeds”
- “Wash the hat” / “Fan the hat”
- “Let Fern sing” / “Check the green page”

Weak:

- “Keep going” / “Try again”
- “Be kind” / “Be mean”
- “Look here” / “Look there” when both immediately show the same image
- two buttons that lead to the same next scene

### 4.2 Choice language

- Two options per scene.
- Four words or fewer per option.
- Start with a concrete verb where possible.
- Name the object or character when ambiguity is possible.
- Never depend on a child remembering which of two identical-looking characters is meant.
- Avoid repeated generic prompts such as “What happens now?” Use a prompt tied to the
  current goal: “How can they cross?” or “Where should Chompy look?”
- Narrate the prompt if the child is expected to act on it.

### 4.3 Branch state ledger

Every quest requires a state table before prose:

| State | Allowed values | Changed by | Must remain visible until |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goal object | lost / found / dirty / clean | named choice and scene | resolution |
| Companion | Pip / Stone / both | choice | next explicit reunion |
| Prop | carried / dropped / broken | shown action | shown repair or abandonment |
| Location | barn / pond / tree | travel scene | next travel scene |
| Relationship | upset / repairing / restored | consequence | responsive action |

No route may:

- show a recovered item as still missing;
- restore a broken object without a repair scene;
- introduce a companion who was not called or met;
- change weather, time of day or location without a transition;
- use knowledge obtained only on another branch;
- claim an apology or repair happened off-screen.

### 4.4 Merges

Branches may merge to control scope, but:

- not on the immediate next scene;
- not before the chosen action has a visible consequence;
- not if merging erases a broken/found/dirty/carrying state;
- not if the merged text describes only one of the incoming routes.

At a merge, write and test the text against **every incoming state**.

### 4.5 Endings

Every ending MUST:

- resolve or consciously change the opening goal;
- include the protagonist;
- show the result in the image;
- differ materially from other endings;
- avoid a stated moral;
- offer “Read again” and “Finish.”

Changing only the final adjective does not make a new ending.

## 5. Reading levels

Age is guidance; actual placement follows demonstrated reading ability.

### 5.1 Level A

**Reader:** ages 4–6, low-level/early English
**Purpose:** confidence, one clear action, strong image support, simple positional and CVC
language

Hard limits:

- 1 line per page;
- 6 words per line;
- 6 words per page;
- 5–10 scenes on a route;
- no dialogue;
- no subordinate clauses;
- no semicolon, colon, dash or internal comma;
- no abstract moral language;
- no more than 30% multisyllabic word types;
- choice labels no longer than 4 words.

Writing rules:

- Use present tense.
- Prefer subject–verb–object.
- Repeat a useful sentence frame with one meaningful changing element.
- Keep pronoun reference obvious; repeat a name if needed.
- Use concrete nouns and visible verbs.
- One goal, one main problem, one primary prop.
- Art may clarify the event but must not be required to guess an untaught word.

Example frame:

> The hat is in the mud.

Not Level A:

> Muddy wondered whether the hat might be somewhere behind the old barn.

### 5.2 Level B

**Reader:** ages 5–7, low-to-mid English
**Purpose:** longer causal sequence, simple dialogue, contrasting wants and repair

Hard limits:

- up to 2 lines per page;
- up to 8 words per line;
- up to 14 words per page;
- 6–12 scenes on a route;
- dialogue on no more than half of pages;
- no subordinate clauses in the current product standard;
- no more than 35% multisyllabic word types;
- choice labels no longer than 4 words.

Writing rules:

- Use present tense narration.
- Allow short, character-specific dialogue.
- Maintain one main goal and at most one supporting goal.
- A mistake should produce a consequence and a repair.
- Use conjunctions sparingly and only where the sentence remains easy to parse.
- New vocabulary must be inferable from action, briefly supported in audio or revisited.

Example:

> Chompy sees the full basket.
> “Lunch for all,” says Sunny.

### 5.3 Level C

**Reader:** ages 7–9, mid-to-high English
**Purpose:** richer character contrast, mystery, inference and multi-step cause

Hard limits:

- up to 3 lines per page;
- up to 9 words per line;
- up to 22 words per page;
- 8–14 scenes on a route;
- no more than 40% multisyllabic word types;
- choice labels no longer than 4 words.

Writing rules:

- Use present tense narration in Story Quests for consistency.
- Allow concise dialogue with distinct voices.
- Permit one main goal and clearly subordinate steps.
- Ask the reader to infer motive or cause from staged evidence, not missing information.
- Seed every magical rule before it resolves the plot.
- Track objects, clues and knowledge across branches.
- Use vivid words, but only when they do precise work.

Example:

> Gold light leaks under the stone.
> Dewdrop presses one ear to the water.
> “It is awake,” she says.

### 5.4 Early decodable track

“Early” is a phonics track, not simply an easier Level A.

- Declare the exact grapheme–phoneme correspondences available.
- Declare common exception words.
- Every word must be decodable from that scope or explicitly taught as an exception.
- Do not add an attractive but out-of-scope destination word.
- Do not make pictures a guessing strategy.
- Keep the story coherent despite the restricted code.

## 6. Human writing and the anti-slop standard

### 6.1 Signs of generated or generic prose

Reject or rewrite:

- “Everyone smiled.”
- “They learned that…”
- “From that day on…”
- “It was a magical adventure.”
- “Together they knew anything was possible.”
- “A warm feeling filled…”
- “His frown softened.”
- “She almost smiled.”
- “They carefully made sure everyone was safe.”
- repeated “What happens next?” prompts;
- three near-synonymous adjectives;
- a sudden group celebration after a trivial action;
- a character cameo that has no want or causal role;
- an object with a face, wink, smile or personality for no established reason;
- purple glow, sparkles or “ancient magic” used only to create atmosphere;
- an apology, friendship speech or moral replacing a concrete repair;
- conflict announced and resolved on the same page;
- characters explaining feelings the picture and action should show;
- adults’ therapy or risk-assessment language in a child’s mouth;
- perfectly polite characters who never make mistakes;
- arbitrary alliteration that distorts natural speech;
- “and then” plotting with no causal chain.

### 6.2 Human-specificity test

Each story needs at least three details that could belong only to this character and world:

- Grumpy quietly empties a basket instead of giving a friendship speech.
- Stone’s huge call makes a small frog hide.
- Speedy outruns the map and must recover it.
- Muddy finds a clean hat and creates a mud problem.

If the names can be swapped with characters from another world without changing the plot,
the story is generic.

### 6.3 Emotion rule

Stage emotion through:

- action;
- distance;
- posture;
- gaze;
- what a character protects, drops, avoids or repairs;
- one short line of dialogue.

Do not diagnose the emotion for the child when the scene can show it.

### 6.4 Moral rule

The character may change behaviour. The narrator may not state the lesson.

Good:

> Flint shuts the lantern. He listens.

Weak:

> Flint learned that listening is always better than rushing.

## 7. Story creation workflow

### Gate 1 — Canon

- Select world and reading level.
- Select canonical character IDs.
- Attach model sheets and scale lineup.
- State each character’s want and behavioural engine.
- Resolve any existing visual conflict before drafting.

### Gate 2 — Story spine

- Write the one-sentence spine.
- Name the initiating event, goal, obstacle, attempts, genuine failure, turning point,
  climax and outcome.
- Name the concrete spine object.
- Prove this plot could not be reassigned unchanged to another cast.

### Gate 3 — Branch map

- Draw the complete page graph.
- Add the state ledger.
- List every ending.
- Check every path reaches an ending.
- Check every choice edge and every merge against all incoming states.
- Keep all route lengths inside the level stamina limit.

### Gate 4 — Text

- Draft to the band limits from the first sentence.
- Read aloud.
- Remove explanation, hedging, generic emotion and repeated prompts.
- Confirm each page follows because of the previous scene.
- Confirm every declared target word appears.

### Gate 5 — Art

- Create or attach the locked sequence sheet.
- Make page art from the final text and state ledger.
- Compare adjacent pages at full size.
- Compare every character against the master, not just the previous generated page.
- Reject wrong species, size, colour, clothing, prop state, weather or style.

### Gate 6 — Audio

- Freeze text.
- Generate word-for-word narration.
- Run file, text-hash, pronunciation, pacing and intelligibility checks.

### Gate 7 — Child playtest

Without explaining the plot, ask children:

- Who is the story about?
- What do they want?
- What went wrong?
- Why did that happen?
- What did your choice change?
- How did it end?

Observe rather than rescue. Confusion is a defect signal.

### Gate 8 — Adult review

A teacher/editor checks:

- reading level;
- decodability where claimed;
- vocabulary;
- cause and effect;
- world and character canon;
- accessibility;
- image–text match;
- audio match;
- cultural and social representation;
- replay value.

## 8. Editorial scorecard

Score each category from 0–4.

| Category | 0 | 2 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Character | interchangeable | recognisable but thin | unmistakably this character |
| Goal | absent | present but vague | early, concrete and sustained |
| Causality | event list | mostly linked | every beat earns the next |
| Obstacle | none | mild delay | meaningful failed attempt and escalation |
| Choices | cosmetic | some consequence | visible, remembered and replay-worthy |
| Ending | stops | resolves plot | resolves plot with distinct emotional landing |
| Language band | wrong | technically fits | fits and still sounds natural |
| Read-aloud voice | flat/generic | competent | rhythmic, specific and memorable |
| Visual continuity | contradictory | mostly consistent | model-perfect across every scene |
| Image–text match | conflicting | generally aligned | every image advances comprehension |
| Audio | absent/stale | present | exact, clear and fully verified |
| Delight | none | pleasant | funny, tense, moving or wondrous enough to reread |

Release threshold:

- no category below 3;
- at least 38/48 overall;
- Character, Causality, Language band, Visual continuity, Image–text match and Audio must
  each score 4 for a flagship release.

## 9. Final preflight

Before marking a story “ready to ship,” answer **yes** to every item:

- [ ] I can state the protagonist’s goal in one concrete sentence.
- [ ] The opening creates the goal quickly.
- [ ] The protagonist causes events through choices and attempts.
- [ ] At least one attempt genuinely fails or complicates the goal.
- [ ] Every scene follows causally from the route before it.
- [ ] Every choice has two plausible, distinct consequences.
- [ ] No merge erases prior state.
- [ ] Every ending resolves the opening goal and differs materially.
- [ ] The story does not state its moral.
- [ ] The dialogue sounds like these characters, not generic “nice” characters.
- [ ] The prose fits the exact Level A/B/C or decodable limits.
- [ ] Repetition supports fluency without replacing decoding.
- [ ] Every target word is useful, present and supported.
- [ ] Every character uses a world-qualified canon ID.
- [ ] Species, palette, markings, clothing and scale match the master.
- [ ] Weather, location, prop and relationship states remain continuous.
- [ ] Every image depicts the exact text and route state.
- [ ] No image contains malformed text, extra limbs, duplicate bodies or faceless living
      characters.
- [ ] Every scene has exact word-for-word narration.
- [ ] The full route graph, every edge and every ending pass automated checks.
- [ ] A child can retell who wanted what, what went wrong and how it ended.
- [ ] An editor would choose to read it again even without the educational wrapper.

If the final answer is not yes, the story is not finished.
